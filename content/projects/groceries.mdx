## What it is

A grocery app for one household — your stores, your products, your list — shared live
between two people and backed by your own Supabase project rather than someone else's
service. One invitation shares everything, forever, including whatever you add next week.

## Local-first, on purpose

Every read the UI does is local and every write lands locally first. That is not an
optimisation; it is the whole premise. The moment this app is most needed is standing at
the back of a shop with one bar of signal, so the list can never be waiting on a network.
Core Data is the local store and `SupabaseSyncEngine` moves changes in and out around it.

`SyncBookkeeper` marks local writes in exactly one place — observing the context's
will-save rather than asking each of several dozen write paths to remember a flag — and
deletions leave a tombstone, because deleting the local object does nothing to the row in
Postgres and the next pull would bring it straight back.

## Everywhere you actually shop

The interesting part of this app is how many surfaces it has, and the rule that governs
all of them: **the phone is the only writer.**

- **A Live Activity for the trip.** Start shopping and the list moves to the Lock Screen
  and Dynamic Island — the next few items in aisle order, each one tappable, so the phone
  never has to come out of your pocket between aisles. Pin a shop on the map and it starts
  itself when you walk in.
- **An Apple Watch app.** The list on your wrist in aisle order, every row a tap to check
  off, and it keeps working with the phone in a bag on the other side of the shop.
- **A Home Screen widget**, and "Hey Siri, add milk to Groceries."

The widget reads a JSON snapshot rather than running its own Core Data stack. The Live
Activity's buttons run a `LiveActivityIntent` back in the app's process. The watch posts
check-offs back and the phone applies them. So every write in the household goes through
one repository in one process, and your partner's phone cannot tell whether an item was
checked off from a wrist, a Lock Screen, or the app.

The watch is that discipline stretched across two devices, which introduces the one real
problem: a tap has to land now, and the phone may be minutes away from hearing about it.
So the watch draws its unconfirmed check-offs over the phone's copy and retires each one
when a payload arrives that already agrees with it — convergence by agreement rather than
by clock, because two devices' clocks differ by more than the round trip takes.

## Lists that match how you shop

Items route to the store you usually buy them at. Lists group into categories in the order
you walk that store, each with its own colour. Adding an item means picking from products
you have bought before rather than searching a generic database. Dietary rules are tags
plus rules you write — gluten-free is just the one that ships enabled.

**Recipes** import by pasting a link: the app reads the schema.org recipe data cooking
sites already publish for search engines. No AI and no scraping — the page is fetched
straight to your phone. Ingredients are parsed and matched against your catalog; confident
matches link automatically and the rest are yours to confirm, because a wrong silent match
means the wrong thing in the cart. That confirmation is remembered.

## On-device intelligence, off the typing path

Suggestions come from three sources, cheapest first, and the language model is never
between a keystroke and a suggestion: your catalog first, then a bundled grocery
vocabulary, and only then — for a word neither has ever seen, after typing stops for
450ms — Apple Intelligence, running on the phone through FoundationModels.

Because the model is a guesser, `ItemIntelligenceSanitizer` sits between it and the UI: the
aisle has to be one your household actually has, the unit one the app writes, and the name
has to still refer to what you typed. It can fix "brocoli"; it cannot answer "Cabbage" when
you typed "kimchi". Anything that fails is dropped, so a bad guess degrades to *no
suggestion* rather than to the wrong thing in the cart.

Nothing about your groceries leaves the device — which is the only reason the feature
belongs in an app whose premise is "no server to run".

## Testing what can be tested

All the grocery logic lives in `GroceryKit`, a platform-neutral Swift package with no
Apple-framework dependencies, so it tests anywhere Swift runs, including Linux and CI.
Three Python checks gate the things that fail silently rather than loudly: the Core Data
model against what the sync engine assumes, weak-linking of frameworks newer than the
deployment target, and privacy manifests against the code that is actually there.
